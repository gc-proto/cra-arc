Get ready to submit a lead

Do not put yourself in danger or break the law to make a submission or to get information.

If you fear for your safety, contact your local law enforcement because the CRA cannot provide physical protection.

Under the Canadian income tax system, income tax obligations to Canada are based on a taxpayer’s residency status, not by citizenship or immigration status.

Information you need

Make sure you have sufficient, specific, and credible details about the alleged international tax non-compliance.

Submissions may be rejected for:

  • lack of supporting documents
  • dated information
  • insufficient details
  • unlikeliness of CRA recovering more than $100,000 in federal taxes
  • the tax non-compliance is not international

Examples

Complete submissions

Nicole reports on her ex-spouse

Nicole knows that, during her marriage, her ex-spouse was breaking Canadian tax laws by hiding offshore assets from the CRA. The assets include foreign properties, bank accounts, and businesses in the U.S. and other countries.

A few months after her divorce, Nicole heard about the CRA’s Offshore Tax Informant Program (OTIP), which may pay eligible individuals for information about major international tax non-compliance. She decided to send detailed information about her ex-spouse to the OTIP. She sent T4 slips and notices of assessment that appeared to have been fabricated by her ex-spouse.

The CRA found additional information to support Nicole’s allegations that her ex-spouse was hiding assets offshore, as well as a domestic bank account with a large balance.

Based on this information, the OTIP referred the information to the CRA’s audit team. They audited  Nicole’s ex-spouse which resulted in a reassessment of over $100,000 in federal taxes, as well as interest and penalties.

Nicole may be eligible to receive a reward between 5% and 15% of the federal taxes that are collected for this case.

If you have information similar to Nicole’s or other information about major international tax non-compliance, you could receive a financial reward. To find out if you are eligible, go to Report offshore tax cheating – Eligibility.

Simon reports clients hiding money in another country

Simon is a financial planner working outside of Canada and has information about many Canadian resident individuals and companies with accounts in an offshore bank. He sent this information to the OTIP to help the CRA combat international tax non-compliance. Simon may be eligible for a reward as a non-resident informant.

Simon gave recent and specific information to the OTIP on over 50 Canadian account holders. He provided supporting documents and enough detail, including the name and location of the bank, to help the CRA clearly identify the individuals and companies and the amount of money in their offshore accounts.          

This information helped the CRA audit 25 people for possible offshore tax avoidance which resulted in reassessments assessed the individuals and companies involved for large amounts of unreported income. They now owe significant taxes, penalties and interest.

The money collected will help the Government of Canada pay for public services such as health care, education and infrastructure.

If you have information similar to Simon’s or other information about major international tax non-compliance, you could receive a financial reward.

Disclaimer: This example is based on a fictitious submission.

David and Joanne report offshore tax schemes

David and Joanne worked and lived abroad for six months. During this time, they did business with two Canadians residents who turned out to be involved in a tax scheme to hide offshore assets from the CRA.

Upset by the unfairness of these schemes, David and Joanne sent information to the OTIP about rental properties and investments owned by the two Canadian residents, as well as supporting documents such as property records and bank statements. They also sent copies of tax returns that the two Canadian residents had filed in the other country.

Because the alleged activities were ongoing, and David and Joanne provided supporting documents, the CRA was able to identify the Canadian individuals and prove they had unreported offshore bank accounts, corporations, and other assets. This information helped the CRA auditor in issuing a more complete assessment of their unreported income.

These CRA audits resulted in a reassessment of millions of dollars, as well as hundreds of thousands of dollars in penalties and interest. When the CRA collects on that debt, David and Joanne will receive a reward in exchange for their valuable information.

Incomplete submissions

Elaine reports on a family member

Nine years ago, Elaine’s father sold several properties worth millions of dollars and hid the proceeds overseas. Elaine knew at the time that these assets were not reported on her father’s Canadian tax returns but she did not report this to the CRA until after her father passed away. She completed the OTIP form and sent it to the CRA with supporting documents.

In this case, the allegations and supporting documents are difficult to substantiate because a long time has passed, so the OTIP rejects her submission.

It is important to send your submission as soon as you learn of any potential international tax non-compliance. Waiting too long could prevent the CRA from acting on the lead and ultimately cost the informant a potential reward.

Jerry reports on the CEO of the company he works for

Jerry is a hard worker and believes in following the rules. One day in the cafeteria, he overhears the CEO and the company accountant discussing how they hid $10 million dollars in an overseas account. Knowing that this is wrong, Jerry sends a lead to the OTIP.

Unfortunately, Jerry does not have any documents that can corroborate what he overheard. Without supporting documents and only minimal details, the OTIP is unable to verify Jerry’s allegations. As a result, it rejects Jerry’s submission.
